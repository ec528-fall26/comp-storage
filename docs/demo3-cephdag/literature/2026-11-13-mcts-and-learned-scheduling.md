## Conclusion

The four families the brief asked about all depend on one of two things that the lab does not have yet. Search-based methods (MCTS, beam, rollout) and every learned method trained in simulation need a forecast that ranks plans correctly. Per-decision methods also need a decision cost far below the time between dispatches. Today the forecast misprices exactly the quantities that separate good plans from bad ones on this lab. Writes take 12–15 ms measured against 0.3–2.2 ms modelled, 6–22 ms of dispatch overhead per task is not modelled, and that overhead tracks bytes moved. Plans that move data are therefore systematically underpriced. The literature names this failure: lookahead over an imperfect model degrades and compounds the model's error [17][18]. ML performance predictors hit an irreducible-variability floor [43], and better prediction accuracy often buys little end-to-end [44].

Taken one family at a time, this means:

- **Learned cost models (F3) come first.** They are not the most interesting option, but they gate all the others. A small residual model of per-task overhead, fitted to the lab's existing per-task logs and judged by whether the simulator ranks the five policies the way the lab measured them, is cheap (about 1–2 days, no lab time) and decides whether anything trained or searched in simulation can transfer. The Halide autoscheduler shows that the pattern works: a hybrid symbolic-plus-learned cost model driving a classic tree search [39]. 3Sigma shows that the forecast should be a distribution, not a point [45].
- **LLM-evolved heuristics (F4) are the best-supported route to a better policy.** They have the closest systems precedent: AlphaEvolve evolved Borg's scheduling heuristic against a simulator built from historical snapshots, validated it on unseen data, deployed it, and recovers 0.7% of fleet compute. Google chose it over deep RL for interpretability and ease of deployment [51]. It fits the lab's constraints: the evolved artefact is a scoring function costing microseconds per decision, and the lab already has a fast simulator and a real testbed to check against. The open-source tooling has reproduced systems results in hours for tens of dollars, and its authors list overfitting and reward hacking as the failure modes [53].
- **MCTS (F1) is worth one controlled experiment, and only in its modern form.** The lab's MCTS is textbook UCT with 48–128 simulations over about 25 root actions, a fresh tree at every decision, horizon-12 rollouts that end in a tail estimate, and a final choice by lowest mean. That is the regime where UCT is known to do badly: the root decision has a simple-regret objective [19][20], the budget is about the size of the action set [11], and an equal-budget DAG study found a weak tree losing to randomized restarts [2]. Gumbel root sampling with Sequential Halving [11] is the version to test. It should also get a value at the leaves that is better than the tail estimate [14], tree reuse [1][21], and search only on contentious decisions [20], all in simulation first and against randomized restarts at an equal budget.
- **Learned policies (F2) are the most interesting option for Aakash but the least likely to pay off before the final report.** If they are pursued, train them by expert iteration from a search teacher [9][35] in the calibrated simulator, and deploy them one-shot: one forward pass yields priorities and placements that a list scheduler executes [23][24][25]. Per-dispatch neural inference does not fit the decision budget on 10,000-task graphs. Offline RL on the lab's own logs is not worth it, because a few deterministic policies give poor state-action coverage, and coverage is what such methods need [37].

I checked one cheap alternative against the lab's own numbers and it does not pay: a learned selector that picks among the existing policies per workload (Bao-style [41]). On the skew results the best fixed policy (data-local) is within 0–15% of the per-intensity winner and 4% from it in geometric mean (§6). A selector could gain at most that much, so the headroom has to come from better plans.

**Ranked shortlist:** (1) a calibrated residual cost model with a policy-ranking fidelity gate, (2) an LLM-evolved dispatch scoring function, (3) Gumbel-MCTS with Sequential Halving and selective invocation, (4) expert iteration distilled into a one-shot policy. For Aakash to write himself, (3) followed by (4) is one arc: a small "Gumbel AlphaZero for cephdag" that starts inside the existing `Search::choose` in `lib.rs`. Expert iteration was introduced on Hex [9].

## 1. What the lab's evidence demands of any planner

Every method below has to clear two bars that the lab has already measured.

**The decision budget.** A serial coordinator with W single-slot workers and mean task time τ receives about W/τ decisions per second. Every millisecond of planning delays a free worker by a millisecond. In the DROID pilot, beam-4 spent 3.2 s planning 472 tasks (about 6.8 ms per decision), kept the data local, and still finished 15% behind data-local's 39.1 s (`PILOTS.md`). Planning explains at most 3.2 s of that roughly 6 s gap; the rest is the plan. The brief adds that beam-4 spends about 80 s planning a 9,700-task DROID batch (about 8 ms per decision) and is about 20% slower than data-local. I did not find that run in a results file and took the figure from the brief; `PILOTS.md` estimates a 79-shard wave at about 9,300 tasks. Greedy spent 60.1 s of a 72.6 s DROID run planning 272 tasks (`LAB-VALIDATION.md`). A per-decision method therefore has a few milliseconds per decision on large graphs. A once-per-run method should aim for about 1% of makespan, about 0.4 s on the DROID pilot (my rule of thumb, not a measurement).

**The forecast.** The native simulator is contention-aware, but its per-task overheads are wrong in the direction that matters. Writes are underpriced by about 10 ms per task and dispatch overhead (6–22 ms) is absent. That overhead correlates with bytes moved (Spearman ρ 0.93–0.95) much more than with planning time (`2026-11-12` review). The compute model under-predicted 64 MiB tasks 3–9× before the 2026-11-13 recalibration (`LAB-VALIDATION.md`), and eight workers share one DRAM bus (`PILOTS.md`). On the skew workload, which policy wins changes with arithmetic intensity. HEFT is last everywhere because its link model ignores contention (`RESULTS-skew.md`). A planner that searches harder over this forecast will prefer plans that move data, because those are the plans the forecast underprices.

**What has already been tried.** Native MCTS-128 lost to beam-4 and HEFT on both large workloads (85.45 s against 58.54 s; 87.69 s against 74.80 s; `AGENTS.md`, 2026-11-03). The October runs found greedy, CP-SAT, rollout and MCTS within about 1% of each other. The 2026-11-12 review bounded the headroom with a fluid LP and recommended a once-solved LP plan, a drain-time-aware locality rule, and pipelined dispatch. This review asks what MCTS and learning add on top of that.

## 2. MCTS for a scheduling decision

### 2.1 What the lab's MCTS does, and where the literature says it is weak

The planner in `lib.rs` builds candidate actions from the first eight ready tasks × free workers × output-placement variants. It ranks them by a one-step profile (predicted seconds, then bytes), keeps the top 24, and adds a wait action when something is running (`actions`, lines 832–856). It then runs UCT with c = √2 on a normalised mean (line 962) for 48 iterations by default (128 in the baseline campaign), within a 40 ms budget. Rollouts last 12 dispatches: 80% of the time they take the best of the first four ready tasks by one-step profile, the rest uniformly random, with writes kept local. A rollout that does not finish the graph is scored with `s.tail(g)` (lines 887–925). The action taken is the root child with the lowest mean (lines 1016–1028), and the tree is discarded after every decision.

With about 25 root actions and 48–128 simulations, each root child is visited 2–5 times. The tree hardly grows below the root, so the search is close to flat Monte Carlo over a pre-pruned list, scored mostly by the tail estimate. Five strands of the literature bear on this design.

**The root decision is a simple-regret problem.** UCT minimises cumulative regret, but a planner is judged only on the action it finally takes. Tolpin and Shimony replace UCB at the root with sampling policies that have lower simple regret, and their two-stage SR+CR scheme beats UCT empirically [19]. Hay, Russell, Tolpin and Shimony frame "which simulation to run next" as a Bayesian selection problem and argue that this is more appropriate than the bandit framing [20]. Gumbel AlphaZero/MuZero puts this into practice. It samples k root actions without replacement from the prior (Gumbel-Top-k), splits the simulation budget among them by Sequential Halving, and acts on the completed Q-values. It is built so that the search improves on the prior even when not every root action is visited. The authors test it with as few as 2 simulations on 19×19 Go (362 actions), and report "significantly improve[d] prior performance when planning with few simulations" [11]. The lab's search, with n ≈ k, sits in exactly that regime.

**Large action spaces need a prior or widening, not a hard cut.** The lab's cut to the top 24 by one-step profile is a fixed prior that has no way of recovering an action ranked 25th. Progressive widening adds children as a node's visit count grows. Double progressive widening extends the idea to stochastic transitions and shows a deceptive problem on which plain UCT fails even with progressive widening [16]. Sampled MuZero does policy evaluation and improvement over sampled action subsets in a principled way [12]. Joint compute and output placement on 8 nodes multiplies the action count by 8 for every output, so this matters more here than in job-shop work.

**Single-player search behaves differently from game search.** Schadd's single-player MCTS work on SameGame found that many small randomized-restart searches can beat one large search, and that spreading search time evenly over consecutive positions beats spending it at the start [65]. The equal-budget DAG study is the sharpest result for this lab [2]. At 2,048 evaluated schedules, its preregistered MCTS was 2.88% worse than randomized-restart list scheduling (95% CI [−3.62%, −2.15%]). The cause was a selection rule that locked onto the first-expanded child, so its trials stayed correlated, while the restarts drew their gains from the tails. Swapping in textbook UCT removed most of the deficit (−0.26%, CI crossing zero) but did not reverse it. Restarts won on sparse, irregular DAGs, regular layered DAGs showed no difference, and dense ones left no room to improve. The scale was small (8–32 tasks). Policy-based self-competition (GAZ PTP) handles single-player problems by planning against a historical version of the policy rather than a scalar baseline. It beats single-player Gumbel AlphaZero variants on TSP and job-shop with half the simulation budget [13].

**Evaluation dominates.** Kemmerling et al. varied the components of neural MCTS for job-shop scheduling. The evaluation phase had the largest effect, and the best agents initialised node values from a learned value function and also ran rollouts [14]. In the lab's search, most of the value signal comes from `s.tail(g)` after 12 dispatches on graphs of 128–10,000 tasks. The quality of that tail estimate, not the tree policy, is probably what limits it. The fluid LP bound from the 2026-11-12 review (4–27 ms to solve) is the obvious candidate for a leaf value. It is a principled lower bound on remaining makespan under measured rates.

**Rollout policies can be adapted during search.** Nested Rollout Policy Adaptation adjusts the rollout policy by gradient ascent at each level of a nested search, and set records in deterministic puzzles [7]. A 2025 adaptive-bias variant for flexible job-shop beats other MCTS methods but is "still far from known upper bounds" on large instances [8]. Those are offline optimisations with minutes of budget, not per-dispatch planners.

### 2.2 Planning when planning time is wall time

Three ideas make MCTS anytime and cheap. **Tree reuse** keeps the subtree under the chosen action as the next root. Spear does this [1], and it is one of eight real-time enhancements Soemers et al. evaluated in general video-game playing, most of which gave statistically significant gains individually [21]. In the lab the next decision's real state differs from the simulated child, because finish times differ, so reuse needs a state-matching rule or a reset when the deviation is large. **Metareasoning about whether to search** [20] matters most here. Searching pays only when the decision is contentious and the candidates differ in quantities the model prices well. Under the lab's forecast, differences smaller than about 10 ms per task are noise. Two plans that differ mainly in how many writes they make cannot be told apart by search. Two that differ in gigabytes crossing node 0's link can be. **Budget allocation over time**: spend evenly across decisions [65], or concentrate search at the few moments when many tasks become ready at once.

### 2.3 Planning with an imperfect model

UA-MCTS starts from a given but imperfect model, shows that the discrepancy can degrade standard MCTS significantly, and modifies all four phases to steer search towards transitions whose outcome is more certain [17]. ProTuner, which applies MCTS to Halide scheduling, argues that beam search over a learned cost model compounds error at every step, because the model must judge partial, "not meaningful" schedules. MCTS compares complete schedules instead, and mixing real execution measurements with the cost model helps further; MCTS beat beam search on 16 benchmarks [18]. Both carry over to the lab. The uncertain transitions are remote reads and writes over a contended link, and the most robust simple policy, data-local, is the one that avoids them. A search that prices uncertainty would rediscover data-local's caution unless the forecast is fixed. That is the argument for shortlist item 1.

### 2.5 How MCTS maps onto the lab

Actions: (task, worker, output targets) or wait, with the prior given by the existing one-step profile or a learned scorer. State: what the coordinator knows (ready set, running tasks and their predicted finish times, object locations, per-link queues, per-node backlog), which the native `State` already holds. Budget: a few milliseconds per contentious decision, none on uncontentious ones. Leaf value: the calibrated tail or the fluid LP bound. The control at equal budget is randomized-restart list scheduling, built by perturbing the data-local or HEFT priority and taking the best of K.

## 3. Learned schedulers

### 3.1 Per-decision RL policies

Decima remains the anchor. It uses a GNN over job DAGs, trained by policy gradient in a Spark simulator calibrated with profiled task durations, and its decisions take under 15 ms each. On a 25-node Spark cluster it cut average job completion time by at least 21% against hand-tuned heuristics, and by up to 2× under high load. It was trained for at least 50,000 iterations with 16 parallel workers [22]. The closest 2026 match to this lab's problem is Miao et al., a heterogeneous-GNN PPO scheduler that jointly chooses the task, the compute node and the storage replica in a disaggregated data centre, with feasibility masking and a multi-objective reward. Its abstract reports only simulation, and its headline 54.2% reduction is relative to the policy's own initial training stage, not to a baseline scheduler [26]. It is an SSRN preprint and I read only the abstract.

For this lab the obstacle is cost per decision. At 15 ms per decision, a 9,700-task DROID batch would spend about 145 s on inference, more than beam-4's 80 s, which already costs about 20%. Per-dispatch neural policies fit the 128-task synthetic graphs but not DROID.

### 3.2 One-shot policies: inference once per graph

Jeon et al. replace episodic RL with a one-shot encoder that samples node priorities, which list scheduling turns into schedules. The run time is comparable to fast classical heuristics, and the schedules beat neural and non-neural baselines [23]. Qi et al. improve the training of such one-shot DAG schedulers (comparable-antichain identification and a dense reward) [24]. WeCAN produces task-to-pool scores in one forward pass and analyses the optimality gap that the list-scheduling "generation map" introduces [25]; the previous review covered it. This is the deployable form for the lab: one forward pass per graph or per wave produces per-task priorities and per-output placement preferences, and the existing dispatcher executes them, so planning costs O(graph) once.

### 3.3 Device and operator placement

Mirhoseini et al. trained a sequence-to-sequence placement policy against measured step times and beat hand-crafted placements [28]. GDP generalised across graphs, reporting 16% over human experts and 9.2% over prior art with 15× faster convergence, and fine-tuned to graphs of more than 50,000 nodes [30]. Placeto learned iterative placement improvements with graph embeddings, needed up to 6.1× fewer training steps, and transferred to unseen graphs within a family [29]. GiPH, also an improvement policy, reports up to 30.5% lower completion times and up to 3× faster search than other search-based placement policies on heterogeneous devices [31]. The counterweight is Baechi. An algorithmic placer (m-SCT/m-ETF-style list scheduling under memory limits) generated plans 654× to 206,000× faster than learning-based placers, with step times comparable to expert placements [32]. For output placement on this lab, the improvement-policy framing of Placeto and GiPH is the useful idea: start from data-local and learn which outputs to move. Baechi is the reminder that a good list heuristic is the bar.

### 3.4 Imitation and self-labelling: learning from a search teacher

Expert iteration splits the work in two: tree search plans, a network generalises the plans, and the network then guides the next round of search. It beat REINFORCE on Hex, and the final agent, trained from scratch, defeated MoHex 1.0 [9]. AlphaZero, MuZero [10] and Gumbel MuZero [11] are the later forms. In single-player constrained settings, MuZero with a self-competition reward cut VP9 video size by 6.28% at equal quality in libvpx [15]. That is a production-codec result where the reward was "beat your own past performance on this instance", which suits the lab well, since makespans vary by an order of magnitude across intensities.

The cheapest form of imitation needs no RL. SLIM samples β schedules from its own generative model, takes the best as a pseudo-label, and trains on it [35]. On very large job-shop instances (200×20 to 500×40), sampling 512 schedules took 31–343 s and gave a lower average makespan than CP-SAT with a one-hour limit [35]. For the lab, "imitate the best of K randomized restarts in the simulator" is SLIM with a list-scheduling sampler. It needs a simulator and a feature encoder, not reward shaping.

### 3.5 Offline RL from logged schedules

Offline-LD trains job-shop dispatching with conservative Q-learning on only 100 constraint-programming solutions, and beats online RL; adding noise to the expert data matched or improved results [36]. CDQAC finds that offline RL for job-shop "depends more on state-action coverage than on the quality of individual trajectories": data from a random heuristic beat data from a genetic algorithm, and 1–5% of the dataset sufficed [37]. PORL pretrains and then adapts offline, and its advantage grows as data quality falls [38]. The lab has thousands of logged dispatches, but they come from a handful of deterministic policies on a few graph families. By CDQAC's own finding that is the wrong kind of data. Logs generated in a calibrated simulator by randomized policies would be better, and at that point the method is no longer learning from the real system.

### 3.6 Neural combinatorial optimisation for job-shop

L2D learned dispatching rules with a GNN over the disjunctive graph and generalised to larger instances [33]. L2S learned an improvement heuristic that outperforms state-of-the-art DRL-based methods by a large margin [34]. These are the strongest learned job-shop lines, but job-shop has no data placement or link contention. Their transferable idea is the representation: a graph over tasks and machines with masked actions.

### 3.7 How learned policies map onto the lab

Training needs the calibrated simulator, graph generators matching the skew, sweep, competing and DROID families, and a GPU machine (aakashpc or aakashpc2). The state is the native `State` encoded as a heterogeneous graph of tasks, nodes and links. The actions are per-task priorities and per-output placement logits, decoded by the existing list dispatcher. The budget is one forward pass per wave. The teacher is either best-of-K restarts (SLIM) or Gumbel-MCTS (expert iteration).

## 4. Learned cost models feeding classic planners

The lab's evidence says the forecast is the bottleneck, and the systems literature points to the same kind of remedy: keep the analytical model and learn its error.

**Hybrid models drive search well.** The 2019 Halide autoscheduler runs a beam search whose cost model "combines symbolic analysis with learning". It trained that model on hundreds of thousands of randomly generated programs and schedules, and produced schedules almost twice as fast as the previous autoscheduler without autotuning [39]. AutoTVM learned statistical cost models to guide search over billions of tensor-program variants and transferred them across workloads [40]. ProTuner shows the trap. A cost model trained on complete schedules misjudges partial ones, and beam search over partial states compounds that error [18]. The lab's beam and MCTS both score partial plans with a tail estimate, so they are in that position.

**Predict distributions.** 3Sigma found that 8–23% of runtime predictions in three production traces were off by a factor of two or more. Scheduling on full runtime distributions came close to the end-to-end performance of a hypothetical perfect predictor [45]. Lotaru predicts per-task, per-node runtimes for workflows with Bayesian linear regression from microbenchmarks plus a down-scaled local run, and gets uncertainty estimates as a by-product [46].

**Know the floor.** Fu et al. tested 6 ML models on 13 real applications and found that 12 have inherent performance variability that fundamentally limits prediction accuracy [43]. Kuchnik et al. found that better prediction accuracy can give small end-to-end gains in common cluster deployments, and that workload non-stationarity erodes predictors over time [44]. The lab's run-to-run drift (80 to 110 s across campaigns) is that non-stationarity.

**Watch for trace bias.** CausalSim shows that traces collected under one policy bias a simulator that is then used to evaluate another, and corrects for this by learning latent system conditions from randomised trials. It cut simulation error by 53% and 61% on average against expert-designed and supervised baselines [42]. In the lab, per-task overhead correlates with bytes moved, so a residual model fitted mostly on data-local runs will extrapolate badly to plans that move data. The training set needs HEFT and stealing runs, which move gigabytes, and ideally a deliberately randomised placement run.

**Train for the decision, not the error.** Decision-focused learning trains predictors through the downstream optimisation; the JAIR survey benchmarks eleven methods on seven problems [47]. The Kubernetes study reached the same conclusion from the other side. Learned node-scoring models with improving R² still lost on top-1 placement accuracy to a single-feature heuristic (free CPU: 74–84% against 65–66%), and the authors attribute this to training with pointwise regression rather than a ranking loss [49]. For the lab, the residual model should be judged, and if possible trained, on whether it ranks the candidate actions and the five policies correctly. Per-task MAPE is not the target. Augmenting a simulator with a learned correction term, rather than replacing it, is also the approach of [48] for network digital twins.

**Mapping onto the lab.** The existing per-task records (read, compute, write and dispatch times, bytes, source and target node, concurrent tasks per node and transfers per link) give a regression target: measured minus modelled stage time. Fit a small linear or quantile model on the sweep runs and test it on the skew runs. Then replay the five policies' measured settings through the corrected simulator and compare the predicted order with the measured order. No new lab time is needed.

## 5. LLM-driven heuristic discovery for systems

**Mechanism.** An LLM proposes program edits to a policy function inside a fixed skeleton. An evaluator, here the simulator, scores each candidate on a training set of instances, and an evolutionary database keeps the best and most diverse programs to prompt the next round. FunSearch introduced this and found online bin-packing heuristics that beat widely used baselines, and stresses that it searches for programs that describe how to solve a problem rather than for solutions [50]. OpenEvolve is the open-source reimplementation of AlphaEvolve that most systems groups use [52].

**Evidence in systems.** AlphaEvolve's data-centre result is the anchor. An early version evolved Borg's scheduling heuristic from the one in production, using a simulator built from historical workload and capacity snapshots. The result was checked on an unseen test set, rolled out fleet-wide, and recovers on average 0.7% of fleet compute that would otherwise be stranded. It was chosen over a deep-RL approach for interpretability, debuggability, predictability and ease of deployment [51]. The ADRS paper applied OpenEvolve to a range of systems problems and reports up to 5.0× runtime improvements and 26% cost reductions. On multi-region spot scheduling it found about 30% greater savings than an expert baseline. Most case studies took a few hours and a few dollars to tens of dollars (Table 1: for example 8 h, 300 iterations, ≤ $10). The authors list overfitting to the feedback traces and reward hacking of the evaluator as recurring failures, and evolved against a 30% subset of traces to limit overfitting [53]. PolicySmith synthesised instance-specific caching heuristics that beat baselines on open traces, and congestion-control policies that run in the Linux kernel [54]. Glia uses a multi-agent LLM workflow to design routing, scheduling and autoscaling for an LLM-inference cluster at human-expert level in much less time [55]. Tagliabue runs the same generate-and-verify loop against an open-source scheduler simulator for a data platform and reports preliminary throughput gains [56].

**Making the search robust.** Exposing the LLM to the instances where its heuristic does worst, explaining why, and specialising by input region gave about 28× better worst-case performance than FunSearch [61]. Giving the LLM event-level simulation traces of its worst run raised a dynamic production and AGV scheduling score from 62.49 to 78.61. The final policy beat rolling-MILP, rule-based and metaheuristic policies on all 100 matched seeds [62]. EoH [57], ReEvo [58] and MCTS-AHD [59] vary the search over programs. MCTS-AHD keeps every generated heuristic in a tree, so that heuristics doing poorly for now can still be developed. That is MCTS over programs, which may interest Aakash more than another population method.

**Caution.** A benchmark of four LLM-based evolutionary program-search methods across nine LLMs found no method consistently best, and a simple (1+1) baseline was competitive on most problems [60]. The reported gains depend heavily on the LLM, the problem, and the evaluator.

**Mapping onto the lab.** This fits the lab unusually well, for three reasons. The evaluator exists: the native simulator, plus a final check on the cephdag8 lab. The skeleton exists: `Policy.choose(state)` in the Python policies, or better a pure function `score(task, worker, output_target, features)` called by a fixed list dispatcher. And the result costs microseconds per decision, which meets the decision budget. The skew result, where the winner changes with intensity, is the kind of regime-dependent rule an evolved scorer can express and a hand-written one usually does not. The risk is equally specific. Against an uncalibrated simulator, evolution would exploit the mispriced writes and dispatch overhead, the reward hacking [53] warns about. Item 1 has to come first. A new policy is also Aakash's call (`AGENTS.md`, "Reserved for him").

## 6. The strongest competing view: simple heuristics usually win

The evidence that search and learning do not beat simple heuristics is strong and recent, and it covers each family.

- **MCTS against an equal-budget simple search.** A weak tree loses to randomized restarts, and textbook UCT only ties them; restarts win on sparse, irregular DAGs [2]. Heatmap-guided MCTS for large TSP lost to LKH-3, and a simple baseline heatmap beat the learned ones [64]. Schadd found many small restarts beat one big search [65].
- **Learned policies against a random or trivial control.** On a homogeneous Slurm trace no masked DRL policy beat a masked uniform-random control. On a heterogeneous trace MaskablePPO only matched the heuristics' waiting time within ±10% [27]. A trivial free-CPU rule beat learned Kubernetes scorers on top-1 placement accuracy [49]. GPU-based AI solvers for maximum independent set lost to the CPU solver KaMIS, and some lost to degree-greedy, even in distribution [63]. Baechi's list scheduler placed graphs 654–206,000× faster than RL placers at comparable step time [32].
- **Prediction does not convert into scheduling gains.** Better accuracy brought small end-to-end gains [44], and variability sets a floor [43].
- **LLM evolution has no consistent winner** [60], and it is prone to overfitting and reward hacking [53].
- **The lab itself.** The October planners finished within about 1% of each other; MCTS-128 lost on both large workloads; on DROID, beam-4 lost 15–20% to data-local.

I computed one more data point from `RESULTS-skew.md` (median wall seconds, cs mode, five policies at four intensities). The per-intensity oracle totals 175.6 s (greedy-wait at 1, stealing at 16, beam-4 at 128, data-local at 1024). The best single policy, data-local, totals 178.9 s. Data-local's ratio to the winner is 1.15, 1.01, 1.00 and 1.00, a geometric mean of 1.04. **A policy selector could recover at most about 4% here**, which is why I do not recommend one. Data-local leads the fixed policies (stealing 185.4 s, greedy-wait 196.0, beam-4 203.8, HEFT 251.2).

The reasonable reading is not that nothing beats data-local. The 2026-11-12 review found 18–34 s of fluid-bound headroom on the older lab, and it comes from idle workers and from moving the wrong objects. Choosing better among the existing policies, or searching harder over the same mispriced forecast, does not touch that headroom. A method has to change the plan (LP, an evolved scorer, a learned one-shot plan) and has to be judged against data-local and randomized restarts with planning time charged. Nobody has bounded the headroom on cephdag8 yet; doing that is part of item 1.

## 7. Ranked shortlist

| # | Approach | Smallest discriminating experiment | Cost to implement | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Calibrated residual cost model and a policy-ranking fidelity gate** [39][45][42][47] | Offline, no lab time. Fit measured-minus-modelled stage time (read, compute, write, dispatch) as a function of bytes, local/remote, node concurrency and link concurrency on the sweep runs; test on the skew runs. Report per-stage error before and after, and Kendall τ between simulated and measured policy order at each of the 4 skew intensities (5 policies each). Also compute the fluid bound per intensity. **Prediction:** the uncorrected simulator misorders HEFT against stealing or data-local at one or more intensities, and the corrected one orders at least 14 of the 16 adjacent policy pairs correctly. If the corrected mean τ stays below about 0.6, no simulator-trained method below can be trusted, and the LP plan with a cheap dispatcher remains the answer. | 1–2 days of analysis code (Python over existing per-task logs) | Inherent variability [43] and policy-induced trace bias [42]: residuals learned mostly from data-local runs may not extrapolate to heavy-movement plans |
| 2 | **LLM-evolved dispatch scoring function** (FunSearch/AlphaEvolve via OpenEvolve) [50][51][52][53][61] | In simulation after item 1 passes. Seed with data-local and the drain-time locality rule; evolve `score(task, worker, target, features)` inside a fixed list dispatcher for about 100–300 iterations against skew at 4 intensities plus competing and DROID waves, holding out one intensity and one DROID wave. Then 3 paired lab repeats of the best candidate against data-local on skew i=1 and i=128 and one DROID wave. **Success:** ≥ 5% better than the best existing policy on held-out simulated cells, and ≥ 5% better than data-local on the lab in at least two of the three, with no loss beyond noise in the third. | 2–3 days to build the evaluator harness; a few hours and ≤ $20–50 of LLM calls per run (ADRS's reported range) | Reward hacking of the forecast's errors [53]; overfitting to the graph families; needs his yes as a new policy |
| 3 | **Gumbel-MCTS with Sequential Halving, a better leaf value, tree reuse and selective invocation** [11][19][20][14][2] | In simulation. Same 128-task skew graphs (i=1, i=128) and one 472-task DROID shard set. Arms at equal simulated transitions: current UCT-MCTS, Gumbel-SH root (k = 8–16, n = 16/32/64), Gumbel-SH with LP-bound leaf value, randomized-restart list scheduling, beam-4. Charge measured planning time. **Success:** Gumbel-SH at n = 32 beats UCT-MCTS-128 and randomized restarts by ≥ 3% simulated makespan at no more than beam-4's planning time. | 4 | **Expert iteration distilled into a one-shot policy** [9][35][23][24] | In simulation. SLIM-style first: sample K = 64 randomized list schedules per training graph, label with the best, train a small GNN (or a linear scorer first) to output priorities and placement logits; evaluate one-shot on held-out graphs against data-local, best-of-64 restarts at equal wall time, and the LP plan. Upgrade the teacher to Gumbel-MCTS (item 3) only if SLIM's student is within 2% of its teacher. | 2–3 weeks; GPU training on aakashpc or aakashpc2 | Sim-to-real; small graph families; the strong negative results on learned schedulers [27][49][63]; unlikely to be ready before the final report on 2026-12-09 |

**Not recommended:** per-dispatch RL in the Decima style (inference cost on 10,000-task graphs, §3.1); offline RL on the lab's own logs (coverage, §3.5); a learned policy selector (≤ 4% headroom, §6); learning a MuZero-style dynamics model (the lab has a simulator, and a learned model would add error rather than remove it).

**Why this order.** Item 1 is the cheapest, uses existing data, and decides whether items 2–4 are worth running. Item 2 has the best systems precedent and zero runtime planning cost. Item 3 is cheap because the code exists, but the lab's evidence counts against lookahead. Item 4 is the most interesting and the most expensive.

## 8. For Aakash: what to write himself, and where to start

The project that fits his interests is items 3 and 4 as one arc: a Gumbel AlphaZero for cephdag, built up in stages that each produce a result.

1. **Gumbel root search in the existing planner.** Start in `cephdag/native/src/lib.rs`, `Search::choose` (lines 927–1029). Replace UCT at the root with Gumbel-Top-k sampling from a prior and Sequential Halving over the n simulations [11]. Keep UCT, or Gumbel's deterministic non-root selection, below the root, and act on completed Q-values instead of the lowest mean. The prior can at first be a softmax over the existing one-step profile in `actions` (lines 832–856), which already ranks candidates; this replaces the hard top-24 cut with sampling. Add the equal-budget control from [2], best-of-K randomized list schedules, in the same file so the comparison is fair by construction.
2. **A better leaf value.** Replace `s.tail(g)` at rollout cutoff with the fluid LP bound, or with a residual-corrected tail once item 1 exists. Kemmerling et al.'s finding that evaluation dominates [14] predicts that this matters more than the tree policy.
3. **Tree reuse and when to search.** Keep the subtree under the chosen action when the real state matches the simulated child within a tolerance [1][21]. Search only when the top candidates' predicted costs differ by more than the forecast's error band, and the difference comes from link bytes rather than per-task overhead [20].
4. **Expert iteration.** Log (state, Sequential-Halving visit distribution, outcome) from the simulator; train a small policy and value network on aakashpc2; use it as the prior in step 1 [9][11]. ExIt's original domain was Hex, so the loop will be familiar.
5. **Distil to one shot.** Train a one-shot priority and placement head on the same targets [23][24]. One forward pass per DROID wave is the only form of the learned policy that fits the decision budget on 10,000-task graphs.

The project file reserves new scheduling policies for him, so all of these are his to write. Item 1 of the shortlist (the residual model and fidelity gate) is analysis code and could be delegated. Item 2's evaluator harness could be delegated, but the policy it evolves needs his yes. If he would rather have MCTS in the LLM loop, MCTS-AHD is MCTS over programs [59]: the same algorithm with heuristics as the states.
